    "## Core channels and plates\n",
    "\n",
    "The core has four groups of plates: one hot plate, 300 normal plates, 90 low-power plates and 10 wider unheated bypass channels. Plates of a group behave alike, so each group is modelled once: one channel and, for the heated groups, one plate. Later, in the flow graph, the group's edge carries the plate count as its `signify` weight, so the junctions see one channel's flow times the number of plates.\n",
    "\n",
    "The example package provides two short constructors for the core. `make_channel` lays 10 axial cells along the 0.7 m plate, takes the channel's geometry from `plate_pipe` (a rectangle 70 mm wide with the coolant gap as its depth, heated on both wide sides) and returns a `ChannelAndContacts` for a heated group, which exchanges heat with walls on both sides, or a plain `Channel` for the bypass. With `p.friction == \"regime\"` it gives the channel a friction factor that blends the laminar and turbulent laws between Reynolds numbers 2000 and 5000. `p.htc` picks the wall heat-transfer law; the default, `\"regime\"`, which composes laminar, turbulent and natural convection, is chosen because the transient ends in natural circulation. `stop_at_saturation` makes a heated channel end a transient with a named error once its coolant reaches saturation, the edge of what the single-phase model can describe.\n",
    "\n",
    "`make_fuel` builds one plate as a `Fuel`: 10 axial cells, eight meat cells between two cladding cells on each side, and a cosine power shape along the plate. Its `share` scales that shape so the plate takes its fraction of the core power: the group's peaking factor over the sum of plate count times peaking factor, $1.6 / (1 \\cdot 1.6 + 300 \\cdot 1.0 + 90 \\cdot 0.5)$ for the hot plate."

    "## Junctions, pool and flapper\n",
    "\n",
    "Seven junctions hold the loop together: the top and bottom of the pool, the plenum under the core, the inlet and outlet of the decay tank, and the split and merge of the two pump legs.\n",
    "\n",
    "The pool beside the core is a square column of water as tall as the core and holding 300 m³, so its side is about 21 m. It is a plain `Channel` with 10 cells, built from an `EffectivePipe.rectangular` square. Its edge will run from the pool bottom to the pool top, so positive flow in it goes upward; `pressure_diff`, the channels' pressure law, takes positive flow as downward, so the pool gets it with gravity reversed.\n",
    "\n",
    "The flapper joins the pool bottom to the plenum. It stays shut while the flow returning from the heat exchanger is above `p.flapper_fraction` (30 %) of the nominal core flow and opens below it, then behaves as a local loss of coefficient 5 over 0.05 m². A flapper stuck shut is modelled by an opening threshold no flow can reach.\n",
    "\n",
    "The trip sensor `LowFlowTrip` is a calculation the example package adds: it watches the same returning flow, latches at 85 % of nominal, and half a second later inserts the rods, worth 10 dollars, over one second. Its worth is given in absolute reactivity, the dollars times the total delayed-neutron fraction."

    "## Pipes, tank and pump legs\n",
    "\n",
    "The library has no single pipe constructor; a pipe is a `Friction` for its wall friction, one `Bend` per elbow, and a `Gravity` for its change of height. The function below assembles those three for a 0.4 m pipe; it is the same as the example package's private helper `_pipe_parts`. Three pipes use it: the primary pipe from the plenum down 4.3 m to the decay tank, with two elbows; the basement pipe down the remaining 3 m to the pumps, with one; and the riser, 7.3 m back up from the heat exchanger to the pool bottom, with two. A positive `disposition` is a drop along the flow, so the riser's is negative.\n",
    "\n",
    "The decay tank holds 20 m³ in a horizontal square column 5 m long; `horizontal_pressure` is `pressure_diff` without gravity.\n",
    "\n",
    "Each pump leg is four components in series. The `Pump` imposes half the nominal core flow and leaves its head to the solve. A `ResistorFromKnownPoint` gives the pump its curve: a parabolic loss that takes 60 % of the estimated loop head at the nominal leg flow, so the head falls as the flow rises. The `Inertia` is the flywheel, which carries the flow on after the pump loses its drive. The library's `CheckValve` is a quadratic loss forward and a hundred thousand times stiffer backward, so a stopped leg does not carry reverse flow.\n",
    "\n",
    "The heat exchanger returns the water at 38 °C, and a second `ResistorFromKnownPoint` gives it its 0.5 bar loss at the nominal core flow."

    "## The flow graph\n",
    "\n",
    "`FlowGraph` takes the edges of the network, each a `flow_edge` from one junction to another with its components in series, and builds the Kirchhoff calculation that holds every edge's flow and every junction's pressure. An edge's direction is the direction of positive flow: the four core edges run from the pool top down to the plenum, each weighted by its plate count; the pool runs from its bottom up to its top; the flapper from the pool bottom to the plenum; then the primary pipe, the tank, the basement pipe, the two pump legs in parallel and the heat exchanger with the riser back to the pool bottom. That last edge also names the flapper and the trip in `ref_mdot_for`: the flow they watch is the riser's.\n",
    "\n",
    "The keywords say the rest. `inertial_comps` lists the components whose flow has inertia, the flywheels, the channels, the pool and the tank, and `KirchhoffWDerivatives` gives each of them a flow-derivative equation. `abs_pressure_comps` hands the heated channels the absolute pressure, which their saturation temperature needs. `funcs` gives the flapper and the trip the clock. The pressure is pinned at the pool top, at the surface pressure plus the weight of 8 m of water at 38 °C.\n",
    "\n",
    "`check_gravity_mismatch` sums the gravity heads around every closed loop of the graph at zero flow and uniform temperature, and raises if one does not close: a typo in a height shows up here rather than as a phantom flow later. It is silent for this loop. `draw` then shows the graph object `FlowGraph` built, laid out like the loop above: the junctions and, on each edge, its first component, with each arrow pointing the way positive flow runs."

    "## Plates on channels\n",
    "\n",
    "The flow graph knows the channels but not the plates. `symmetric_plate` couples a channel to its plate as if each surrounded the other on both sides, which is how a channel between two identical plates sees them: the plate heats the channel through both walls, and the channel cools both faces of the plate. For the thermal-hydraulic steady state each plate is fed a constant `power`, the full core power, of which its `share` keeps its own fraction. Adding the three plate graphs to the flow graph's aggregator gives the complete thermal-hydraulic system, and `report` lists every calculation with its equations and the inputs that are left unset or provided by external functions. The column to look at is **Missing**: an input there has no source and no default, and the solve would fail or, worse, run on a value nobody chose. For this loop the column is empty. The **Unset** inputs fall back on what their calculations were built with: the bypass, the pool and the decay tank have no walls to exchange heat with, the plates' ends are insulated, and each pump imposes the flow it was built with and leaves its head for the solve to find. Set externally are the plates' power and the clocks of the flapper and the trip."

    "\n",
    "A steady solve needs a starting point, and for this loop a rough one is enough: every temperature at the heat-exchanger outlet, every pressure drop zero, heat-transfer coefficients of 1000 W/m²K, and flows set by the kind of edge: 0.5 kg/s through each core channel (one plate's flow), the full core flow through the pool and the pipes, half of it through each pump leg, and nothing through the flapper.     "## Attaching the kinetics\n",
    "\n",
    "The solve lands where the hand estimates put it: the outlet temperatures within about 0.004 K of the energy balance, the per-plate flows within about 3.4 % of the gap law, and both pumps at about 1.28 bar. With the thermal-hydraulics solved, the constant power gives way to point kinetics. `PointKineticsWInput` takes six delayed-neutron groups, a generation time of 50 µs, temperature feedback and a reactivity controller. The feedback is a reactivity per kelvin on every coolant cell and every meat cell. The core's coefficients, -1e-4 per K for the coolant and -2e-5 per K for the fuel, are split between the groups by plate count times peaking factor, and within a group evenly over its cells. The reference temperatures are the solved ones, so the feedback is zero at the operating point and the reactor starts critical. The controller reads the rods' reactivity from the trip sensor.\n",
    "\n",
    "The kinetics node is a single calculation, and its links are added by hand in a `CalculationGraph`: it reads the temperatures `T` of the heated channels and plates and feeds each plate its `power`. Besides the fission power it takes a `power_input`, the decay heat, which counts from the trip; at the operating point it is 6.6 % of the power. The plates are attached with `symmetric_plate` again, this time without a constant power, and the new graph is added to the same flow graph's aggregator."

    "## Where the numbers come from\n",
    "\n",
    "The reactor of these examples is a generic open-pool reactor of the MTR class, not a particular plant. Its loop is the layout the loss-of-flow literature on such reactors describes: forced flow down through the core, flappers between the plenum and the pool, the pool as the heat sink, a decay tank, two pump legs with flywheels and check valves, a heat exchanger and a riser back to the pool. The numbers are illustrative values for a 10 MW reactor of that class, chosen so that one loop behaves sensibly in every scenario of these pages, not read off a plant. The table shows how they compare with published figures.\n",
    "\n",
    "| Quantity | This case | Published figure | Where |\n",
    "|---|---|---|---|\n",
    "| Core power | 10 MW | the IAEA benchmark reactor is a 10 MW plate-type MTR | IAEA 10 MW benchmark (Aziz 2011; defined in IAEA-TECDOC-233) |\n",
    "| Fuel plates | 391 heated plates in three groups, about 17 elements of 23 plates, plus a bypass group of 10 unheated channels | standard element 23 plates, control element 17 plates (HEU core) | Aziz 2011; TECDOC-467 gives \"23, some less\" |\n",
    "| Coolant gap | 2.2 mm | 2.19 mm (HEU core), 2.217 mm (LEU core) | Aziz 2011 |\n",
    "| Plate | 0.7 m by 70 mm, meat 0.6 mm, cladding 0.5 mm | meat 600 mm long (a few up to 800 mm), 60 or 62.8 mm wide, 0.51 mm thick (a few 0.76 mm); plate 1.27 mm; cladding specified as 0.38 mm; element 7.71 cm by 8.1 cm with 60 cm active length | TECDOC-467; Aziz 2011 |\n",
    "| Core inlet temperature | 38 °C | ETRR-2: 40 °C; TR-2: 17 to 29.5 °C | Doval and Abdou 1999; Sevdik et al. 2007 |\n",
    "| Primary flow | about 242 kg/s, about 880 m³/h, at 10 MW | ETRR-2: 1900 m³/h at 22 MW; TR-2: 750 m³/h at 5 MW | Doval and Abdou 1999; Sevdik et al. 2007 |\n",
    "| Low-flow trip | 85 % of nominal flow, 0.5 s delay, about 2.5 s into the coastdown | ETRR-2: 90 % of nominal, no delay assumed in the simulation; TR-2: flow scram 7 to 9 s after the flow starts to fall | Doval and Abdou 1999; Sevdik et al. 2007 |\n",
    "| Flapper opening | 30 % of nominal flow, about 29 s after the pumps start to lose head | ETRR-2: 68 s simulated, 75 to 76 s measured; TR-2: 25 to 35 s | Doval and Abdou 1999; Sevdik et al. 2007 |\n",
    "| Decay tank | 20 m³, about 80 s of transit at nominal flow | TR-2: 22 m³, 3 min | Sevdik et al. 2007 |\n",
